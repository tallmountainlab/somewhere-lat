Oaxaca smells like a grandmother's kitchen: toasted chiles, chocolate ground with cinnamon, corn tortillas puffing up on a clay comal. It is a city that keeps its traditions close, and nowhere is that clearer than during Día de Muertos, when families welcome back the people they have lost. If there is one place in Latin America built on remembering, it is this one.

Día de Muertos in Oaxaca

Day of the Dead is celebrated across Mexico around November 1 and 2, and Oaxaca is one of the most popular places to experience it. Families build altars (ofrendas) with marigolds, candles, food and photos to welcome back loved ones, and cemeteries fill with visitors at night.

  • Book rooms months ahead. Late October and early November is the city's busiest time of year.
  • Remember that cemetery visits are family gatherings. Ask before photographing people or graves, and keep your voice down.
  • Look for altars and sand tapestries (tapetes) around the city center and in markets.

What to eat

  • Mole, Oaxaca is known as the land of seven moles. Try mole negro at least once.
  • Tlayudas, a large crisp tortilla with beans, cheese and meat, often grilled over charcoal at night.
  • Quesillo, Oaxaca's stringy cheese.
  • Chocolate, ground with sugar and cinnamon and served hot.
  • Mezcal, the agave spirit made in small distilleries (palenques) around the valleys.

The Mercado 20 de Noviembre and Mercado Benito Juárez are the places to start.

Day trips

  • Monte Albán, the hilltop Zapotec city just outside town. Go early before the heat.
  • Hierve el Agua, mineral springs and "petrified waterfalls". Access has occasionally been closed by local disputes, so check before you go.
  • Mitla, known for its geometric stone mosaics.
  • The Tule tree in Santa María del Tule, one of the widest trees in the world.
  • Craft villages such as Teotitlán del Valle (rugs) and San Bartolo Coyotepec (black pottery).

When to go

October to April is dry and pleasant. The Guelaguetza festival in July is the other big event of the year. Oaxaca City sits around 1,550 meters, so evenings can be cool in winter.

Getting there

Oaxaca's airport has direct flights from Mexico City and several US cities. Buses from Mexico City take roughly six to seven hours.

Then and now

Many of Oaxaca's recipes are passed down by heart, not written down, and some moles take days to prepare, just as they did generations ago. The markets still run the way they always have, with women selling chapulines from baskets and men grinding cacao to order. The city has become a famous food destination, but go to a village market on its weekly market day and you will see a tradition that is centuries old.
