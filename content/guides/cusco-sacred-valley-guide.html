Some cities feel like they were built in layers, and Cusco is the clearest example in the world. Walk down a narrow street and the bottom of the wall is Inca stone, fitted so tightly that a knife blade won't slip between the blocks. Above it sits a Spanish colonial balcony painted blue, and above that, a woman in a tall hat leads a llama past a modern café. For generations of travelers, from the backpackers of the 1970s with dog-eared guidebooks to families today, Cusco has been the first real "wow" of a trip to Peru.

Most visitors treat it as a stop on the way to Machu Picchu. Give it three or four days and you will find that Cusco and the Sacred Valley are a destination of their own.

First, the altitude

Cusco sits at roughly 3,400 meters (about 11,150 feet). The Sacred Valley is noticeably lower, around 2,800 to 2,900 meters. Many travelers sleep in the valley for the first night or two, then move up to Cusco. Walk slowly, drink water, go light on alcohol, and try a cup of coca tea, which is offered everywhere. If you have heart or lung conditions, talk to your doctor before the trip.

Cusco highlights

  • Plaza de Armas: the old Inca ceremonial square, now framed by the cathedral and arcades. Come back at night when it is lit up.
  • Qorikancha: once the Incas' most important temple, its walls were said to be covered in gold. The Spanish built the Santo Domingo convent on top, and you can see both layers in one visit.
  • San Blas: the artisans' neighborhood, uphill from the plaza, with steep lanes, workshops and viewpoints.
  • The 12-angled stone on Hatun Rumiyoc street, a famous example of Inca stonework.
  • Sacsayhuamán: the giant zigzag walls on the hill above town. Some of the stones weigh many tons.
  • San Pedro Market: fresh juices, soups and a look at everyday Cusco.

The Sacred Valley

The Urubamba River valley, below Cusco, is lined with Inca terraces, market towns and ruins.

  • Pisac: a hillside Inca site with sweeping terraces, plus a well-known market in town.
  • Chinchero: a weaving village with a colonial church built on Inca foundations.
  • Moray: circular, stepped terraces that may have been used to test crops at different temperatures.
  • Maras salt pans: thousands of small salt ponds on a mountainside, worked by local families.
  • Ollantaytambo: a living Inca town with original street plans, a huge temple hill, and the main train station for Machu Picchu.

The tourist ticket

Many sites, including Sacsayhuamán, Pisac, Ollantaytambo, Moray and Chinchero, are covered by the Boleto Turístico del Cusco, a combined tourist ticket. Partial versions are sold for smaller groups of sites. Qorikancha, the cathedral and the Maras salt pans have their own separate tickets. Prices and what is included change, so check before you buy.

A simple 4-day plan

  1. Fly into Cusco and go straight down to the Sacred Valley. Rest.
  2. Pisac in the morning, then Moray and Maras in the afternoon. Sleep in Ollantaytambo.
  3. Take the train from Ollantaytambo to Aguas Calientes and visit Machu Picchu. See our Machu Picchu planning guide.
  4. Return to Cusco, now better acclimatized, and explore the city and Sacsayhuamán.

Then and now

Cusco was the capital of the Inca Empire, and the Incas called it the navel of the world. After the Spanish conquest in the 1530s, churches and mansions rose on top of Inca palaces, and the city's double life began. In 1950, a strong earthquake damaged many colonial buildings and revealed Inca walls that had been hidden for centuries, including the curved wall of Qorikancha. When the American explorer Hiram Bingham passed through in 1911 on his way to Machu Picchu, Cusco was a quiet mountain town that few foreign visitors ever saw. Today it has an airport and boutique hotels, but every June 24 the Inti Raymi festival still brings the ancient sun ceremony back to Sacsayhuamán.

When to go

The dry season, roughly May to September, has sunny days and very cold nights. December to March is the rainy season, greener and quieter. Bring layers for any month.
