Antigua Guatemala: cobblestones, volcanoes and colonial ruins

Antigua feels like a town that forgot to change the calendar. The streets are paved with uneven stones that rattle every car and bicycle, the walls are painted ochre, rose and sky blue, and at the end of almost every street stands a volcano, perfectly shaped, like a child's drawing. In the mornings you hear church bells and the clatter of a brightly painted "chicken bus" heading out of town, and by evening the whole city smells of wood smoke and fresh tortillas.

Antigua in brief

Antigua was founded in 1543 as Santiago de los Caballeros de Guatemala, and for more than two centuries it was the capital of a Spanish colony that stretched across much of Central America. Then, in 1773, a series of strong earthquakes badly damaged the city. The authorities moved the capital to a new site, today's Guatemala City, and the old capital became known simply as "La Antigua," the old one. Many of its churches and convents were left as ruins, and they still stand today. UNESCO listed Antigua as a World Heritage site in 1979.

What to see

Santa Catalina Arch

The yellow arch over Fifth Avenue North, with a clock on top and Volcán de Agua behind it, is the photo everyone takes. It was built in the 1600s so the nuns of the Santa Catalina convent could cross the street without being seen. Come early in the morning for the clearest view of the volcano.

La Merced

A short walk north of the arch, the Church of La Merced is covered in white and yellow plaster decoration that looks almost like icing on a cake. The ruins of the convent next door hold a huge old fountain.

The ruins

Some of Antigua's most moving places are its ruins: the roofless back of the cathedral on the central plaza, the convent of Las Capuchinas with its round cloister, and the vast church of San Francisco. Wandering through these broken arches, with bougainvillea growing out of the walls, is the best way to feel the city's history.

Parque Central and Cerro de la Cruz

The central plaza is the town's living room, with shoe shiners, fruit sellers and families on benches. For a view over the whole grid of streets toward Volcán de Agua, climb to Cerro de la Cruz, a hilltop cross north of town. Go in daylight and with others.

The volcano hikes

Three volcanoes surround Antigua: Agua, Acatenango and Fuego. Fuego is one of the most active volcanoes in Central America. The most famous hike is an overnight climb of Acatenango, where you camp near the summit and, on clear nights, watch Fuego glow and erupt across the valley. It is a demanding climb with a big elevation gain and cold nights, so go with a reputable operator, rent warm gear if needed and give yourself a couple of days in town to adjust to the altitude first. A gentler option is Pacaya, a shorter hike on a different volcano a bit farther from town.

Volcanic activity can close trails at short notice. Follow local advice and official alerts.

Semana Santa

Holy Week, the week before Easter, is Antigua's most famous time of year. Families and neighborhoods spend all night making alfombras, carpets of colored sawdust, flowers and pine needles on the streets, and processions with huge carried floats walk right over them at dawn. It is unforgettable, but rooms sell out and prices rise, so book months in advance. The processions actually start in the weeks before Holy Week too, on Lent weekends.

Getting there

The nearest international airport is La Aurora in Guatemala City. The drive to Antigua takes roughly 1 to 1.5 hours, longer in heavy traffic. Shared and private shuttles are the easiest option, and most hotels can arrange a pickup.

When to go

Antigua sits at about 1,500 meters (roughly 5,000 feet), so the climate is mild all year: warm days and cool evenings. The dry season, roughly November to April, has the clearest volcano views and the best hiking. From about May to October, afternoon rain is common, though mornings are often bright, and the green hills are beautiful.

Memory laneThen and now

When the capital moved away after the 1773 earthquakes, many families went with it, and for a long time Antigua was a quiet town of coffee farms, half-empty convents and memories of better days. That quiet is exactly what saved it. There was little money to tear things down and build new ones, so the old street grid and colonial houses survived. Look out for the camionetas, the famous chicken buses. Many began life as yellow school buses in the United States, carrying children to class, before being driven south, painted in wild colors and given a second life on Guatemala's roads. It is hard to find a better picture of Antigua itself: something old, loved and still running.

Practical tips

  • Shoes: the cobblestones are tough on ankles. Wear comfortable, sturdy shoes.
  • Safety: Antigua is generally relaxed, but use normal city precautions at night and take registered taxis or shuttles for longer trips.
  • Coffee: the farms around town grow excellent coffee, and many offer tours.
