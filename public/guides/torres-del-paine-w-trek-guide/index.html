Torres del Paine and the W Trek: a planning guide

When to go, how to book campsites and refugios, and how to get to Torres del Paine in Chilean Patagonia.

Patagonia has always been a word that sounds like an adventure. For generations it meant faraway explorers, sheep ranches the size of small countries and wind that could knock you off your feet. Bruce Chatwin's 1977 book "In Patagonia" sent a wave of dreamers south with dog-eared paperbacks in their packs. The towers of Torres del Paine are still waiting for them, exactly where they have always been.

The W Trek in brief

  • Usually hiked in four or five days, covering roughly 70 to 80 kilometers.
  • The three "arms" of the W lead to the base of the Torres, the French Valley and Grey Glacier.
  • You can camp, rent pre-pitched tents, or sleep in refugios (mountain lodges) with meals.
  • The longer O Circuit adds the remote back side of the park and takes about seven to nine days.

Book early: this is the key step

Overnight stays inside the park must be booked in advance. Campsites and refugios are run by a small number of operators, so you may need to book different nights with different companies. For December to February, popular sites sell out months ahead. You also need a park entry ticket, bought online from Chile's park service.

When to go

The main hiking season runs from roughly October to April. December to February has the longest days and the most people. October, November, March and April are quieter but cooler. Patagonian wind can be extremely strong in any season, so pack layers and a waterproof jacket.

Getting there

Fly to Punta Arenas or Puerto Natales, then take a bus or transfer to the park. Puerto Natales is the main gateway town, with gear rental, supermarkets and hotels. Allow a full day each way for travel.

Not a hiker?

You can still see Torres del Paine on day tours from Puerto Natales, by boat across Lago Grey, or from a lodge inside the park with guided day walks.

Memory laneThen and now

The region was settled by ranchers, and the old estancias still dot the landscape. Some have become lodges where you can sleep in a converted shearing shed. Early hikers carried canvas tents, wool sweaters and paper maps, and the park saw only a few thousand visitors a year. It is far busier now, which is why booking ahead matters, but the glaciers, the wind and that first view of the towers at sunrise feel just as wild as they did then.
