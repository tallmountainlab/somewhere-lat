Iguazú Falls: how to see both the Argentine and Brazilian sides

How many days to spend at Iguazú Falls, the Argentine vs Brazilian side, the Devil's Throat, boat rides, border tips and when to go.

You hear Iguazú before you see it. First a low rumble through the trees, then a fine mist drifting over the path, and then the forest opens and there it is: a long horseshoe of white water falling into a cloud of its own spray, with rainbows hanging in the air and swifts darting straight through the curtain. Generations of travelers have come home from here with the same photo, soaked and grinning on a walkway, and it never gets old.

The falls sit on the border between Argentina and Brazil, close to where Paraguay meets them both. Each country has its own national park and its own point of view, and the best trips see both.

The falls in brief

Iguazú is not one waterfall but a chain of roughly 250 to 300 separate drops, depending on how much water the river is carrying, spread along nearly three kilometers of the Iguazú River. The biggest and most famous is the Garganta del Diablo, or Devil's Throat, a U-shaped chasm where much of the river plunges at once. Both parks are UNESCO World Heritage sites: Argentina's Iguazú National Park and Brazil's Iguaçu National Park.

Argentine side vs Brazilian side

Argentina: up close

Most of the falls are on the Argentine side, so this is where you walk among them. Plan a full day. A small jungle train carries visitors to the station for the Devil's Throat, where a long walkway crosses the river and ends right at the edge of the drop. The Upper Circuit follows the top of the falls, and the Lower Circuit takes you down to the base, where the spray is thick. Arrive early to beat the crowds at the Devil's Throat.

Brazil: the big picture

The Brazilian side gives you the panorama, a view across the river to the long line of falls on the Argentine bank. A single walking trail of a little over a kilometer leads to a platform that juts out toward the Devil's Throat from below. Half a day is usually enough. Many people do Argentina first, then Brazil, so the final view pulls the whole picture together.

Boat rides and extras

On both sides, operators run speedboat trips that charge right up to the base of the smaller falls. You will get completely wet, which is the point. Put your phone and passport in a dry bag. There are also gentler rafting trips on the calmer upper river and, on the Argentine side, guided full-moon walks on certain nights. These sell out, so book them as soon as you know your dates.

Where to stay

  • Puerto Iguazú (Argentina): a small, relaxed town with plenty of guesthouses and restaurants, close to the Argentine park entrance.
  • Foz do Iguaçu (Brazil): a larger city with more hotel choice and good access to the Brazilian park and the Itaipu dam.
  • Inside the parks: each side has a hotel within the park, an expensive treat, but it lets you see the falls before the day crowds arrive.

Getting there

Both towns have airports. Flights from Buenos Aires to Puerto Iguazú take around two hours, and Foz do Iguaçu has connections from Brazilian cities such as São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro. Long-distance buses also reach both towns, but the trip from Buenos Aires is roughly a full day.

Crossing the border

Crossing between the two towns is routine, by taxi, bus or with a tour, but it is still an international border. Bring your passport, get your stamps and allow time for queues. Visa rules for Brazil and Argentina depend on your nationality and have changed in recent years, so check the official rules for your passport well before you travel.

When to go

The falls are worth seeing in any season. Water levels are generally higher after the summer rains and lower in the drier months, which changes how the falls look but not how impressive they are. Summer (December to February) is hot and humid, with more visitors. Spring (roughly September to November) and autumn (roughly March to May) usually bring milder temperatures. Expect rain at any time, and pack a light rain jacket either way.

Wildlife

Keep an eye out for toucans, butterflies by the hundreds and the great dusky swifts that nest behind the water. You will certainly meet coatis, raccoon-like animals that roam the paths looking for snacks. They are cute, but they bite and scratch, so do not feed them and keep your food zipped away.

Memory laneThen and now

For the Guaraní people, the falls were born from a story of love and anger: a forest god, furious that the young woman he wanted ran away down the river with a warrior, split the riverbed and created the falls to trap them forever. The first European on record to see Iguazú was the Spanish explorer Álvar Núñez Cabeza de Vaca, in 1541. For centuries afterward, reaching the falls meant long journeys by river and dirt road through the jungle, and only the most determined travelers made it. Today you can fly in, ride a little train and stand on a metal walkway above the Devil's Throat with a coffee in your hand. The roar is exactly what Cabeza de Vaca heard.

Practical tips

  • Tickets: buy park tickets online when possible. Foreign visitors pay a higher rate than locals, and payment methods vary by side.
  • What to wear: quick-dry clothes, sandals or shoes that can get wet, sunscreen and insect repellent.
  • Two parks, two days: trying to see both sides in one day is possible but rushed. Give each its own day if you can.
