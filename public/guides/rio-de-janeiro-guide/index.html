Rio de Janeiro: beaches, bossa nova and the view from Sugarloaf

What to see in Rio de Janeiro, from Christ the Redeemer and Sugarloaf to Copacabana, Ipanema and Santa Teresa, plus safety tips, Carnival and when to go.

For many of us, Rio arrived first as a song. Somewhere in a parent's record collection was "The Girl from Ipanema," and when that soft bossa nova guitar started, the room seemed to fill with sea breeze. Rio is still that song: a city where green mountains drop straight into the ocean, where people in flip-flops play volleyball at sunset, and where the wavy black-and-white sidewalk of Copacabana stretches on like a postcard from the 1970s.

The big sights

Christ the Redeemer

The statue of Christ with open arms stands on Corcovado mountain, about 700 meters above the city, and was completed in 1931. Go up by the little cog train through the Tijuca forest, or by van. Visit early in the morning or late afternoon, and check the weather. When clouds cover the summit, you may see nothing at all.

Sugarloaf Mountain

Two cable cars climb in stages to the top of Pão de Açúcar, the granite peak at the mouth of Guanabara Bay. The first cable car opened in 1912. The view at sunset, with the city lights coming on below, is one of the best in the world.

Copacabana and Ipanema

Copacabana is the classic, a long curve of sand with a famous wave-patterned promenade designed in its modern form by landscape architect Roberto Burle Marx. Ipanema and Leblon, to the west, are a little calmer, with the Two Brothers mountains at the end of the beach. Rent a chair and umbrella from a beach vendor, buy a cold coconut and stay for the sunset at Arpoador, where people often applaud as the sun goes down.

Neighborhoods to wander

  • Santa Teresa: a hilltop neighborhood of old mansions, artists' studios and bars, served by a historic yellow tram.
  • Lapa: famous for its white arches, an old aqueduct, and for live samba at night.
  • Escadaria Selarón: a staircase between Lapa and Santa Teresa covered in thousands of colorful tiles, the work of artist Jorge Selarón.
  • Centro: grand old buildings such as the Theatro Municipal and the Confeitaria Colombo, a café with mirrors and stained glass from another era.

Nature in the city

Rio has one of the largest urban forests in the world, the Tijuca Forest, with hiking trails and waterfalls. The Botanical Garden is a peaceful escape with royal palms and orchids. For a big view, hike or take a guided trip up Pedra da Gávea or Dois Irmãos, depending on your fitness.

Carnival and New Year's Eve

Carnival takes place in the days before Lent, usually in February or early March. The samba school parades in the Sambadrome are spectacular, but the street parties, called blocos, are where the whole city dances. On New Year's Eve, huge crowds dressed in white gather on Copacabana beach for fireworks. For both events, book rooms months ahead.

Getting there and around

International flights land at Galeão (GIG) airport, roughly 30 to 60 minutes from the beaches depending on traffic. Santos Dumont airport, near the center, handles many domestic flights. The metro is clean and handy for the beach neighborhoods and downtown, and ride-hailing apps are widely used.

When to go

Rio is warm all year. December to March is summer, hot and humid, with the most energy and the highest prices. April to June and September to November are often pleasant, with fewer crowds. Winter, from June to August, is mild and drier.

Memory laneThen and now

In 1808, the Portuguese royal family fled Napoleon's armies and moved their court to Rio, which for a while became the capital of a European empire. Rio remained Brazil's capital until 1960, when the government moved to Brasília. In the late 1950s and early 1960s, young musicians in Ipanema and Copacabana mixed samba with jazz and created bossa nova. "The Girl from Ipanema," written by Antônio Carlos Jobim and Vinicius de Moraes, became one of the most recorded songs in the world. The bars where those songs were born have changed names, but the sunsets over Ipanema are exactly as golden as the music promised.

Staying safe

  • Take only what you need to the beach, and keep your phone out of sight on busy streets.
  • Use official taxis or ride-hailing apps, especially at night.
  • Ask your hotel which areas to avoid, and visit favelas only with a reputable local guide.
