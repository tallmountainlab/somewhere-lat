The Galápagos Islands: how to plan a first trip

Remember the nature documentaries we watched as kids, the narrator speaking softly while a giant tortoise lumbered across the screen? Many of them were filmed in the Galápagos. It is one of the few places on earth where that childhood wonder comes back exactly as it was. Sea lions sleep on park benches, marine iguanas pile up like little dragons on black rocks, and a blue-footed booby will look at you with total indifference from two steps away.

The Galápagos are a group of volcanic islands in the Pacific, roughly 1,000 kilometers off the coast of Ecuador. Almost all of the land is a national park, and visiting takes a little more planning than most trips, but nothing else is quite like it.

Cruise or land-based?

  • Cruise: you sleep on a boat that sails at night between islands, so you see more remote places. It is the classic way, and usually the most expensive. Book well ahead.
  • Land-based: you stay in hotels on the inhabited islands and take day trips by boat. It is more flexible and often cheaper, and good for anyone who gets seasick.

The main islands

Santa Cruz

Home to Puerto Ayora, the biggest town, and the Charles Darwin Research Station, where you can learn about the giant tortoise breeding program. In the highlands, you can watch wild tortoises grazing in the fields. Walk out to Tortuga Bay, a long white beach where marine iguanas sunbathe.

San Cristóbal

The town of Puerto Baquerizo Moreno has a waterfront full of sea lions. Boat trips go to Kicker Rock (León Dormido), a split rock rising from the sea, popular for snorkeling with sea turtles and, sometimes, sharks.

Isabela

The largest island, quieter and wilder, with flamingos in the lagoons, volcano hikes and penguins near the coast.

Getting there

Flights to the Galápagos leave from mainland Ecuador, from Quito (usually with a stop in Guayaquil) or from Guayaquil. They land either at Baltra, next to Santa Cruz, or on San Cristóbal. Before you fly, you will go through an inspection of your luggage, since bringing seeds, fruit or animals to the islands is restricted, and you must buy a Transit Control Card at the airport.

On arrival, you pay the Galápagos National Park entrance fee. The fee for foreign visitors was raised significantly in 2024, so check the current amount before you go.

Wildlife rules

  • Stay at least two meters away from animals, even when they come to you.
  • Never touch or feed them, and do not use flash photography.
  • Many visitor sites can only be visited with a licensed naturalist guide.
  • Stay on the marked trails and take all trash with you.

When to go

The islands are worth visiting all year. From roughly December to May, the water and air are warmer and the sea is often calmer, with some rain. From roughly June to November, cooler currents bring misty garúa days, rougher seas and especially rich marine life. Each month has its own wildlife highlights, from sea turtle nesting to booby courtship dances, so if you have a dream animal, look up the best season for it.

Memory laneThen and now

For centuries, the islands were a stop for pirates, whalers and sailors, who took thousands of giant tortoises aboard as food for long voyages. On Floreana Island, sailors in the late 1700s set up a barrel where they left letters for passing ships to carry home. Visitors still do the same at Post Office Bay today: you leave a postcard and pick up one addressed near your home to deliver by hand. In 1835, a young Charles Darwin arrived on HMS Beagle, and what he saw here helped shape his ideas about evolution. Ecuador created the national park in 1959, and the islands were among the very first places named to UNESCO's World Heritage list. Tortoise numbers have been slowly recovering, and a postcard from Post Office Bay is still one of the most charming souvenirs in the world.

Practical tips

  • Money: Ecuador uses the US dollar. ATMs exist in the main towns, but bring some cash.
  • Seasickness: boat transfers between islands can be bumpy. Bring remedies if you need them.
  • Pack: reef-safe sunscreen, a hat, a light rain jacket and shoes that can get wet.
