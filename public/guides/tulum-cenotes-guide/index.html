Tulum and the cenotes: Maya ruins by the sea and swimming in the jungle

How to visit the Tulum ruins, which cenotes to swim in, getting there from Cancún or Tulum airport, sargassum and hurricane season, and where to stay.

Some travelers still talk about the Tulum of the 1970s and 1980s like a lost paradise: a dirt road, a few palm-thatched cabañas with sand floors and hammocks, candles instead of electricity and a Maya temple standing alone on a cliff above the bluest water they had ever seen. That Tulum has grown up. There are boutique hotels, smoothie bars and traffic now. But walk into the ruins at opening time, with the sea breeze in your face and iguanas sunning on the old walls, and the magic is still very much there.

Tulum sits on Mexico's Caribbean coast in the state of Quintana Roo, south of Cancún and Playa del Carmen. It offers two very different joys: ancient ruins on a beach, and cool, clear swimming holes in the jungle called cenotes.

The Tulum ruins

The walled city of Tulum was one of the last cities built and lived in by the Maya, and it was at its busiest a few centuries before the Spanish arrived. Its position on a cliff above the sea made it an important trading port. The tallest building, known as El Castillo, stands on the edge of the cliff and probably served as a landmark for canoes coming in through a gap in the reef.

The site is now part of a protected national park, and there may be separate fees for the park and the archaeological zone. Go right at opening time, before the heat and the tour buses. You cannot climb the buildings, but paths loop around them, and in some seasons you can walk down to the small beach below the cliff. It may close during turtle nesting or rough weather.

The cenotes

The Yucatán Peninsula is a flat shelf of limestone with almost no rivers on the surface. Instead, rainwater flows through underground caves, and where the ground has collapsed, it opens into cenotes: natural wells of clear, fresh water. The ancient Maya saw them as sacred openings to the underworld. Today they are the best way to cool off.

  • Gran Cenote: close to town, with clear water, cave passages, small turtles and wooden platforms. Popular, so go early.
  • Dos Ojos: two connected cenotes, famous with snorkelers and cave divers for the light beams in the water.
  • Cenote Calavera: a small, open hole you jump into, with a ladder to climb out.
  • Cenotes on the road to Cobá: quieter options that pair well with a trip to the Cobá ruins.

Most cenotes are run by local families or communities and charge an entrance fee in cash. Shower before you swim, and skip sunscreen and insect repellent in the water. They harm the fragile ecosystem, and many sites ask you to rinse off first.

Day trips

  • Cobá: an older, larger Maya city in the jungle, with long white roads called sacbé and a tall pyramid. Renting a bicycle to get around the site is part of the fun.
  • Sian Ka'an Biosphere Reserve: a huge protected area south of Tulum with lagoons, mangroves and boat tours to spot birds, dolphins and turtles.
  • Akumal: a bay north of town known for swimming with sea turtles. Follow the local rules, which may require a guide and life jacket.

Getting there

Tulum has its own international airport, which opened in late 2023 south of town. Many travelers still fly into Cancún, which has more flights, and then drive or take a shuttle or bus south, roughly two hours. The new Maya Train also stops in Tulum.

Where to stay

Tulum town (the pueblo) is cheaper and closer to the cenotes, with lots of taquerías. The beach zone along the hotel road has the famous boutique hotels and beach clubs, at much higher prices. Many people stay in town and bike or take a taxi to the beach.

When to go

December to April is the driest and most popular season. Hurricane season runs from June to November. In recent years, large amounts of sargassum seaweed have washed up along this coast, especially in the warmer months. It varies a lot from week to week, so check recent reports before you book a beach-only trip. The cenotes and ruins are great whatever the seaweed is doing.

Memory laneThen and now

When a Spanish expedition sailed along this coast in 1518, the crew described a city on the shore that reminded them of the cities back home. For centuries after the Spanish conquest, Tulum was mostly left to the jungle. In 1842, the American writer John Lloyd Stephens and the English artist Frederick Catherwood reached the ruins and made drawings that introduced the site to readers far away. Even in the late twentieth century, Tulum was a sleepy stop on a bumpy coastal road, discovered by backpackers who slept in hammocks for a few dollars a night. Today the ruins welcome visitors from all over the world, but the view from the cliff, white stone against turquoise sea, is the same one those first sailors saw.

Practical tips

  • Cash: carry pesos for cenotes, small restaurants and colectivo vans.
  • Sun and heat: bring a hat and water to the ruins, which have little shade.
  • Bikes: a bicycle is the easiest way to get between town and the beach.
