How to plan a trip to Machu Picchu

Tickets, circuits, trains, the Inca Trail and altitude: everything to sort out before you go to Machu Picchu.

Many of us first saw Machu Picchu on the glossy pages of a National Geographic at a grandparent's house or in a school library: the green peaks, the clouds and the stone city that seemed impossible. It has been on bucket lists ever since Hiram Bingham's photographs made it famous in 1911. Standing there in person is one of those rare moments that lives up to the picture. Getting there takes some planning, though. Three things matter most: tickets, transport and altitude.

1. Tickets and circuits

Entry tickets are sold for a specific date, time slot and route (called a circuit) through the citadel. Daily numbers are capped, so buy as early as you can, especially from May to September. Add-on hikes such as Huayna Picchu or Machu Picchu Mountain have their own, smaller quotas and sell out first.

  • Buy from the official Peruvian Ministry of Culture site or a reputable agency.
  • Bring the same passport you used to book. It is checked at the gate.
  • Your ticket time is your entry window, so arrive on time.

2. Getting there

There is no road to Machu Picchu itself. Most visitors take a train through the Sacred Valley to Aguas Calientes, the town below the site, then a 25-minute shuttle bus up the mountain.

3. The Inca Trail

The classic four-day Inca Trail can only be hiked with a licensed operator, and permits are limited per day. For popular months, they often sell out several months ahead. The trail closes every February for maintenance. Shorter one-day versions and alternative treks, such as the Salkantay route, are good options if permits are gone.

4. Altitude

Cusco sits around 3,400 meters (about 11,150 feet), much higher than Machu Picchu itself. Many travelers start in the lower Sacred Valley for a night or two, then move up to Cusco. Take it easy on day one, drink water, go light on alcohol, and talk to your doctor before you travel if you have health concerns.

When to go

The dry season, roughly May to September, has the clearest skies and the biggest crowds. The shoulder months of April and October are a good balance. December to March is the rainy season, with greener views, fewer people and a higher chance of cloud.

A simple four-day plan

  1. Fly into Cusco, transfer straight down to the Sacred Valley.
  2. Explore Pisac market and the Ollantaytambo ruins.
  3. Train to Aguas Calientes, early bus up to Machu Picchu.
  4. Return to Cusco and explore the old town as your body adjusts to the altitude.

Memory laneThen and now

Travelers in the 1970s and 1980s remember a much simpler visit: a slow train, a handful of other visitors and a hotel right at the gate. Some even slept among the ruins, which is unthinkable today. Visits are controlled now to protect the site, and that is a good thing. It means the stones your grandchildren see will look just like the ones on that old magazine page.
